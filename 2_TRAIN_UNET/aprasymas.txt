Metrikos interpretacija:

### 1. Mean Squared Error (MSE)
#### Definition
MSE measures the average squared difference between the clean (ground truth) ECG signal and the denoised signal.
It quantifies the overall error in the denoising process.

#### Interpretation
- **Lower MSE**: Indicates better denoising quality, as the denoised signal is closer to the clean signal.
- **Higher MSE**: Suggests larger errors, meaning the denoising process failed to remove noise or introduced artifacts.
- **Typical Values**: For normalized signals (mean=0, std=1, as in your script), MSE values around 0.01–0.1 indicate
  good denoising, while values >0.5 suggest poor performance. For unnormalized ECGs (e.g., in mV),
  scale depends on signal amplitude.

#### Use in Evaluation
- **Primary Metric**: MSE directly measures reconstruction error, making it a core metric for denoising quality.
- **Limitations**: MSE is sensitive to large errors but doesn’t capture signal structure (e.g., ECG waveform shape).
  A low MSE doesn’t guarantee preserved clinical features (e.g., QRS complexes).
- **Application**: Use MSE to compare models or noise levels. For example, if Model A has MSE=0.05 and Model B has MSE=0.1,
  Model A is better at reconstructing the clean signal.


### 2. Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)
#### Definition
SNR measures the ratio of the clean signal’s power to the noise (error) power in the denoised signal,
expressed in decibels (dB). It quantifies how much noise remains after denoising.

#### Interpretation
- **Higher SNR**: Indicates better denoising, as the signal power dominates the noise power. Positive SNR (e.g., 10–20 dB)
  suggests good denoising.
- **Lower or Negative SNR**: Indicates poor denoising, where noise power is comparable to or exceeds signal power.
- **Typical Values**: For ECG denoising with normalized signals, SNR >10 dB is desirable. For unnormalized signals, 
  interpret relative to input noise levels (e.g., training noise in your case: Gaussian, baseline wander, powerline,
   motion artifacts).

#### Use in Evaluation
- **Complementary to MSE**: SNR normalizes the error by signal power, making it more interpretable across different
  signal amplitudes. A high SNR confirms that the denoised signal retains most of the clean signal’s energy.
- **Clinical Relevance**: High SNR suggests that major ECG features (e.g., P, QRS, T waves) are preserved,
  but it doesn’t guarantee exact waveform fidelity.
- **Application**: Use SNR to assess denoising robustness across noise types. For example,
  if SNR is high for Gaussian noise but low for motion artifacts, the model struggles with transient noise.


### 3. Pearson Correlation Coefficient (Corr)
#### Definition
The Pearson correlation coefficient measures the linear relationship between the clean and denoised signals,
 assessing how well the denoised signal preserves the shape and timing of the clean signal.

#### Interpretation
- **Higher Corr (close to 1)**: Indicates excellent denoising, where the denoised signal closely matches
  the clean signal’s waveform (e.g., QRS complexes align).
- **Lower Corr (close to 0)**: Suggests poor denoising, where the denoised signal lacks similarity to the clean signal.
- **Typical Values**: For ECG denoising, Corr >0.9 is excellent, 0.7–0.9 is good, and <0.7 indicates issues with waveform
  preservation.

#### Use in Evaluation
- **Shape Preservation**: Corr is particularly valuable for ECGs because it evaluates how well the denoised signal
  retains the temporal structure (e.g., P, QRS, T waves), which is critical for clinical analysis.
- **Complementary to MSE/SNR**: While MSE and SNR focus on error magnitude, Corr emphasizes waveform similarity.
  A high Corr with a moderate MSE suggests the denoised signal has the right shape but may have amplitude offsets.
- **Application**: Use Corr to ensure clinical features are preserved. For example, a Corr of 0.95 indicates 
  the denoised ECG is likely usable for diagnosis, while a Corr of 0.6 suggests distorted waveforms.
